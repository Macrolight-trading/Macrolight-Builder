## What should an Ann Arbor, MI business website do in the first few seconds?

**It should make the service, the market, and the next step obvious right away.** If the visitor has to interpret a slogan before they know whether you actually serve Ann Arbor, the page is creating friction instead of removing it.

For most local service businesses, the first screen should answer four questions:

1. What service do you provide?
2. Do you work in Ann Arbor and nearby Washtenaw County communities?
3. Why should the visitor trust you?
4. What should they do next right now?

That is why a headline like **"Ann Arbor Web Design for Local Service Businesses"** or **"Ann Arbor Family Dentistry"** is much stronger than a polished slogan that could belong to any business in any city.

## What should be above the fold on an Ann Arbor service-business homepage?

**The top of the page should make the business feel local, credible, and easy to contact.** Anything less leaves too much work for the visitor.

A stronger above-the-fold layout usually includes:

- A headline tied to the exact service and city
- A short subhead explaining who you help and what makes you credible
- A primary CTA such as call now, request a quote, or book an appointment
- Immediate trust cues such as reviews, years in business, certifications, or process proof you can support
- A secondary CTA for pricing, insurance, financing, or process details when relevant

This is where many service-business websites underperform. They lead with branding language and make the customer hunt for the practical information that actually drives the decision.

## How should an Ann Arbor business measure whether its website is working?

**Measure local visibility and qualified actions separately, then connect the two.** Search Console can show whether priority pages are earning impressions, clicks, click-through rate, and average-position visibility; analytics and CRM records should show whether those visits become calls, bookings, quote requests, and qualified leads. More traffic alone is not proof that the website improved the business.

A practical baseline for an Ann Arbor service business is:

| Measure | What to compare | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Search visibility | Impressions, clicks, CTR, and average position for the homepage, core services, and city pages | Shows whether the right local pages are being discovered |
| Conversion actions | Calls, forms, bookings, and click-to-call actions by page and device | Shows whether visibility creates an opportunity |
| Lead quality | Service requested, location, fit, and eventual close status | Prevents vanity traffic from looking like growth |
| Page experience | Mobile performance and Core Web Vitals on priority pages | Finds friction that can stop an otherwise qualified visitor |

Google's Search Console documentation defines clicks, impressions, click-through rate, and position as core performance metrics. Google also describes Core Web Vitals as a practical user-experience baseline: LCP under 2.5 seconds, INP under 200 milliseconds, and CLS under 0.1 at the 75th percentile. Those thresholds do not guarantee rankings or leads; they are diagnostics to review alongside local relevance, useful content, trust, and the CTA. ([Search Console Performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7042828), [Core Web Vitals](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals))

Use this simple decision rule after a focused change:

1. **Visibility and qualified leads both rise:** keep the structure and improve the offer incrementally.
2. **Visibility rises but leads do not:** inspect trust proof, message match, mobile friction, response speed, and form quality.
3. **Leads rise but visibility does not:** check whether referrals, repeat business, paid campaigns, or seasonality explain the change before calling it an SEO win.
4. **Neither rises:** recheck service intent, local relevance, indexability, internal links, and the conversion path.

## How should you audit an Ann Arbor website before redesigning it?

**Audit the pages and actions closest to revenue before commissioning a redesign.** A useful Ann Arbor website audit checks whether a customer can understand the service, confirm the service area, verify the business, and contact the company without friction—then checks whether search engines can discover those same pages.

Use this five-part pass:

1. **Message match:** Does the title, first paragraph, and main heading clearly name the service and Ann Arbor market?
2. **Local proof:** Are the business details consistent with the Google Business Profile, with reviews or other verifiable trust signals near the CTA?
3. **Money-page structure:** Do priority services have useful pages of their own, with internal links from the homepage and related content?
4. **Answer coverage:** Do the pages answer service-area, timing, pricing-expectation, and next-step questions directly?
5. **Crawl and conversion path:** Can a phone visitor call or submit a short form easily, and can search engines discover important URLs through links and the sitemap?

Google says a sitemap can help search engines discover URLs and crawl a site more efficiently, but it **does not guarantee that submitted URLs will be crawled or indexed**. That is why an Ann Arbor service page needs both technical discoverability and a useful reason to exist. ([Google Search Central: Learn about sitemaps](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/overview))
